# Data Sandbox: layer descriptions

The longer text behind each layer's (i) in the Data Sandbox (/ecoscapes-dst/),
also printed on the full map image. First copied from the map portal.

How to edit:

- Each layer is a `##` heading with its name exactly as the Data Sandbox shows
  it. A heading that matches no layer stops the site build, so a typo shows up
  straight away.
- Under it, the description: plain text, one or more paragraphs.
- Last, a `Source:` line. A link is written `[Name](https://address)`.
- A layer left out here simply has no (i).

# Ecosystem health

## Habitat suitability

The capacity of terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems to provide essential life-cycle resources (food, water, shelter, breeding sites) and suitable living conditions for a species to survive and reproduce. The suitability of a given wildlife habitat is determined by site-specific environmental variables that influence the occurrence and distribution of wildlife species. These include a wide range of biotic and abiotic variables, including the structure and composition of ecological communities within a given biogeoclimatic zone, soil moisture and nutrient levels, topographic position, temperature, slope aspect and solar insolation.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

## Landscape permeability

The physical arrangement and connectivity of habitat patches and natural landscape features (e.g., forests, water bodies) across a landscape, regardless of species-specific behaviour. Structural connectivity is crucial for biodiversity conservation because it offers essential physical pathways that allow wildlife to move freely to access food, shelter, and breeding sites. This movement supports genetic exchange, enhances ecosystem resilience to disturbances, enables species to adjust their ranges in response to climate change, and diminishes the negative effects of habitat fragmentation

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

## Ecosystem resilience

Understanding the adaptive capacity of mountain ecosystems is valuable for assessing areas of the landscape most vulnerable to the cumulative impacts of historic and future disturbances, which could lead to habitat fragmentation and the loss of functional ecosystem services we all depend on for survival. Our assessment of ecosystem resilience builds on the IUCN’s Red List of Ecosystems (RLe). It is based on measured decadal changes in ecosystem health (1985 to present) and on projected forest ecosystem turnover due to climatic shifts over the next 50 years.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

## Ecosystem services

A subjective evaluation of the benefits that current and future generations receive from physical, chemical, and biological processes supporting the functional health and long-term resilience of a natural ecosystem. These include provisioning (food and water), regulating (climate control and disease mitigation), supporting (nutrient cycles and pollination), and cultural services (spiritual and aesthetic value). While our assessment of ecosystem services measures the relative benefits of ecosystem functions in terms of economic value, we acknowledge that all living things have inherent rights and are not to be treated as commodities valued solely for human utility.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Biodiversity index

Regions with anomalously high concentrations of endemic species and ecological communities that are undergoing significant habitat fragmentation and loss of ecosystem health. Hotspots are ranked based on the relative sensitivity of specific ecological communities to environmental stressors and on the uniqueness and rarity of species assemblages that occur within a given area. This multi-layered approach measures the "irreplaceability" of a landscape, allowing conservationists to prioritize habitats where small-scale disturbances could trigger disproportionate, irreversible losses to regional biodiversity.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Conservation priorities

Habitat restoration is the purposeful process of assisting the recovery of degraded, damaged, or destroyed wildland habitats to improve ecosystem health and connectivity and enhance in-situ biodiversity. It is a reactive approach, highlighted by the Global Biodiversity Framework's Target 2, which aims to reverse damage and rebuild resilient landscapes that provide functional ecosystem services. Actions that can be taken at local and regional scales include reforestation of native trees and understory vegetation, eradicating invasive species, and reintroducing native plants and animals.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

# Land cover

## Land cover

Visible terrestrial components of the Earth's surface, which include forests, grasslands, wetlands, alpine areas and various land uses associated with human settlement, such as urban and agricultural areas and linear infrastructure. The spatial arrangement and distribution of these components serve as indicators for monitoring landscape patterns and understanding their effects on ecosystem health and integrity over time, and identifying the underlying causes of biodiversity loss.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

# Pressures

## Cumulative human impacts

A composite indicator that measures the extent and degree to which human impacts reduce or damage ecosystem health and fragment natural wildland habitats. This includes both the permanent loss of wildland habitat for urban expansion (e.g., roads, energy pipelines, and utility corridors) and shorter-term landscape disturbances caused by agriculture, timber harvesting, and mining. The cumulative impact of increased human activities in these areas results in biodiversity loss and disrupts natural patterns of ecological connectivity.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

## Built-up density

The conversion of natural wildland habitats into built environments to accommodate population growth and development. Examples include clearing land for residential neighbourhoods and businesses, agricultural use, resource extraction activities, and associated linear infrastructure such as roads, pipelines, and utility corridors. Direct physical consequences include the loss and fragmentation of wildland habitats and the disruption of natural ecological connectivity pathways. Indirect effects involve altered hydrology (e.g., stormwater runoff) and increased vulnerability to the spread of invasive species and biodiversity loss.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Closeness to trails

Non-motorized recreation is often viewed as benign, but ecological evidence suggests otherwise. High-use hiking and mountain biking trails create a "landscape of fear" for sensitive species. Animals like the wolverine and mountain goat may perceive humans on trails as predators. This triggers a physiological stress response and flight behaviour, often at high energetic cost. If trail use is high enough, it can lead to the functional abandonment of critical habitat, effectively severing connectivity to these life-sustaining areas.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Wildfire hazard

A wildfire conflagration is a severe fire event triggered by a combination of fuel (vegetation), weather conditions (such as high temperatures, strong winds, and drought), and topography (slopes). Wildfires can result in loss of life, destruction of natural habitats, and disruption of ecological connectivity networks. Although some ecosystems are adapted to fire, the increased frequency and intensity of fires can cause lasting changes in vegetation—such as forests turning into shrublands—leading to a decline in the ecosystem's overall health, diversity, and resilience.

Source: [BC Wildfire Provincial Strategic Threat Analysis (PSTA-2025)](https://catalogue.data.gov.bc.ca/dataset/b756c2ac-e753-4771-bbb8-48c7f73d564a)

# Change over 40 years

## Forest change

Loss and fragmentation of forest ecosystems, primarily due to timber harvesting, agriculture, urbanization and wildfire, are critical indicators of habitat disturbance. Removal of tree cover and understory vegetation eliminates homes, food sources, and breeding grounds for many species, leading to habitat fragmentation. The remaining patches of forest cover become isolated, thereby disrupting natural patterns of wildlife movement and the flow of ecological processes. Fragmentation also decreases the amount of suitable habitat along forest edges and increases vulnerability to the impacts of human activity and invasive species.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

## Riparian change

Riparian and wetland habitats (vegetation zones along rivers and streams) are highly vulnerable and critical ecosystems that provide essential ecological services and support ecological connectivity networks for plants and animals. They are hotspots of biodiversity and support the life-cycle needs for a majority of freshwater and terrestrial species. Degradation and loss of these habitats severely impact nutrient cycles, the provision of food and clean water, the regulation of natural flood cycles and the buffering of climate change impacts. Their loss destroys critical habitats for fish, amphibians, birds, and mammals, threatening species extinction.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

## Mesic vegetation change

Plant communities that thrive in soils with higher moisture and nutrient content in areas of groundwater discharge. These include herbs and shrubs that occur in non-forested areas at mid- and lower-slope elevations and along valley bottoms. Fragmentation and loss of these habitats may lead to instability in the flow and provision of ecosystem functions (e.g., pollination, food provisioning), which are essential to a wide range of terrestrial species. It may also weaken an ecosystem's ability to cope with and adapt to disturbances caused by human activity and climate change.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

## Xeric vegetation change

Plant communities that thrive in soils with lower moisture and nutrient levels. These include shrublands in non-forested areas exposed to more sunlight and in drier biogeoclimatic zones of the eastern Coast Mountains. These habitats support species that are uniquely adapted to extreme climatic conditions. Fragmentation and loss of these habitats may cause severe disruption of ecosystem functions, desertification, and reduce the capacity to cope with and adapt to disturbances caused by human activity and climate change.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia](https://terradapt.org/)

# Climate

## Climate refugia (regional)

Areas of the landscape that remain buffered from the most severe effects of climate change, enabling sensitive species to survive locally. They are characterized by stable microclimates, unique hydrological features, or protective topography that provides sanctuary. In the context of biodiversity and landscape ecology, refugia are vital for species survival, acting as "arks" that safeguard genetic diversity and ecological communities. They serve as source populations for future re-colonization of the surrounding landscape, thereby supporting ecosystem resilience and function.

Source: [Jessica Stolar, et al.](https://zenodo.org/records/10603162)

## Climate conservation value

Changes in climate suitability pose a threat to the long-term survival of ecosystems and biodiversity in British Columbia, emphasizing the importance of targeted, climate-aware conservation efforts. Areas that remain relatively undisturbed, indicated by low human footprint, are good candidates for protection and delaying harvesting activities. Conversely, locations affected by human activity but with high potential for climate refugia and connectivity are ideal candidates for ecological restoration, such as wetlands.

Source: [Jessica Stolar, et al.](https://zenodo.org/records/10603162)

# Connectivity

## Connectivity linkages

Modelled pathways of ecological connectivity that provide the most efficient movement corridors for wildlife and gene flow across the widest range of species. These pathways identify areas of least resistance for animals and plants to move between protected areas and other high-value habitats, including core and stepping-stone patches found in fragmented landscapes at lower elevations and in valley bottoms. Mapping these movement corridors helps guide the development of practical conservation solutions that deliver the greatest benefits for vulnerable plants and animals.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

# Species habitat

## Grizzly bear

Grizzly bears (Ursus arctos horribilis) are powerful omnivores with a distinctive shoulder hump, long claws adapted for digging, and fur that ranges from dark brown to blonde, giving them a “grizzled” appearance. Found in remote mountainous and forested regions of British Columbia, grizzlies are solitary and wide-ranging, with home ranges spanning hundreds of square kilometres. They typically feed on seasonal plants and berries, migrating from low-elevation riparian and valley bottom habitats in the spring to high-elevation alpine meadows and avalanche tracks in the summer. In the fall, they often return to lower elevation riparian networks to feast on salmon and other fish. Winter denning occurs primarily in remote alpine or subalpine environments.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia and Nature Squamish](https://terradapt.org/)

## Wolverine

Wolverines (Gulo gulo luscus) are muscular, solitary carnivores known for their strength, endurance, and tenacity. With thick dark fur, pale facial masks, and bushy tails, they resemble small bears more than weasels. Wolverines are adapted to cold, snowy environments and can travel vast distances in search of food. They scavenge on large carcasses and hunt small prey. In BC, wolverines are rare and elusive, with populations concentrated in remote mountainous terrain. Their low reproductive rate and sensitivity to disturbance make them vulnerable to human disturbances and habitat fragmentation.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia and Nature Squamish](https://terradapt.org/)

## Canada lynx

Canada lynx (Lynx canadensis) are elusive, long-legged felines with tufted ears, short tails, and thick fur adapted to cold climates. Their oversized paws act like snowshoes, allowing them to hunt effectively in deep snow. Lynx are highly specialized predators of snowshoe hares, and their populations fluctuate in tandem with hare abundance. Solitary and nocturnal, lynx prefer dense coniferous forests with abundant understory and minimal human disturbance. In BC, they are most common in interior plateaus and northern ranges, with scattered individuals in the Sea-to-Sky region.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia and Nature Squamish](https://terradapt.org/)

## Pacific fisher

The Columbian population of the Pacific fisher in southwest British Columbia (Pekania pennanti, pop 5) is a secretive, forest-dwelling carnivore in the weasel family. With sleek chocolate-brown fur, bushy tails, and agile bodies, fishers are expert climbers and hunters. They prefer mature coniferous forests with complex canopy structure and abundant windfall timber, which they use for denning and hunting. Despite their name, fishers prey on squirrels, snowshoe hares, and even porcupines. In BC, Pacific fishers are rare and declining, with fragmented populations in the southern interior and isolated individuals in the Sea-to-Sky region.

Source: [TerrAdapt-Cascadia and Nature Squamish](https://terradapt.org/)

## Hoary marmot

Hoary marmots (Marmota caligata) are large, social ground squirrels known for their loud whistles (which gave Whistler its name) and communal sunbathing. Their thick silver-grey fur and white muzzle give them a frosted appearance, hence the name “hoary.” Marmots live in colonies and dig extensive burrow systems in alpine meadows, where they forage during the short summer season. They hibernate for up to eight months, relying on fat reserves built during peak forage. In BC, hoary marmots are common in high-elevation habitats, including the Garibaldi and Tantalus ranges.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Coyote

Coyotes (Canis latrans) are versatile canids distinguished by their pointed ears, slender muzzle, and bushy, black-tipped tail often held downward while running. They have a grizzled, gray-brown coat that changes with the seasons. Highly adaptable, they thrive in diverse habitats ranging from forests and grasslands to dense urban environments. As a focal species, they function as mesopredators, helping regulate small-mammal populations and serving as generalist indicators of ecosystem connectivity and adaptability in fragmented landscapes.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Douglas squirrel

The Douglas’s squirrel (Tamiasciurus douglasii) is a lively, vocal tree squirrel with a rusty brown back, pale belly, bushy tail, and a distinct black side stripe in summer. Known for its rapid movements and sharp chatter, it plays a key role in forest ecosystems by dispersing seeds and spores. These squirrels are active year-round, caching cones and mushrooms around the forest. They prefer mature coniferous forests, especially those dominated by Douglas-fir, hemlock, and pine. Their nests, called dreys, are built in tree cavities or branch forks and lined with moss and bark.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Yellow-pine chipmunk

The Yellow-pine chipmunk (Neotamias amoenus) is a small, agile rodent with bold facial stripes and a reddish-brown back. Chipmunks are excellent climbers and often forage in shrubs and low trees. They’re active during the day, storing food in cheek pouches and caching seeds for winter. Yellow-pine chipmunks hibernate in colder months, emerging in spring to breed and forage. Their vocalizations include high-pitched chips and trills used for alarm and territory defence.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Beaver

The beaver (Castor canadensis) is North America’s largest rodent, easily recognized by its large orange incisors, webbed hind feet, and broad, flat, scaly tail. These semi-aquatic mammals prefer freshwater habitats, such as rivers, streams, and lakes, bordered by abundant trees. Beavers are the quintessential ecosystem engineers and a keystone species; their dam-building fundamentally alters hydrology, creating wetlands that filter water, mitigate droughts, and provide critical habitat for a vast array of other species.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Mink

The American mink (Neovison vison) is a sleek, semi-aquatic weasel characterized by a long, tubular body, dark chocolate-brown fur, and a distinctive white chin patch. They inhabit riparian zones, favouring banks of streams, lakes, and marshes with dense cover. As a focal species, the mink serves as an essential bioindicator of wetland health and water quality. As top-level aquatic predators, they are susceptible to pollutants such as mercury, reflecting the overall condition of their aquatic food webs.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Northern red-legged frog

Northern red-legged frogs (Rana aurora) are slender, long-legged amphibians with smooth skin and a reddish hue on their hind limbs. Their eyes are positioned slightly forward, and they often have a faint gold wash on their back. These frogs breed early in the year, sometimes while snow still lingers, laying egg masses in cool, still wetlands. After metamorphosis, juveniles disperse into moist forest habitats, where they forage and overwinter under logs or in root channels. They are sensitive to habitat disturbance and require cool, shaded environments to thrive.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Western toad

Western toads (Anaxyrus boreas) are stout amphibians with dry, bumpy skin and a pale stripe running down their back. Unlike frogs, they walk rather than hop, and their parotoid glands behind the eyes secrete mild toxins to deter predators. These toads breed in shallow wetlands and lay long strings of eggs. After metamorphosis, juveniles disperse into upland habitats, often traveling hundreds of meters from breeding sites. Western toads are active from spring to fall and hibernate underground during winter.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Pacific tree frog

The Pacific tree frog (Pseudacris regilla) is a tiny, vocal amphibian known for its loud nighttime calls and remarkable colour variation, ranging from green to brown, with a dark eye stripe. They are technically tree frogs and possess toe pads for climbing. These frogs breed in shallow wetlands and temporary pools, laying eggs in clusters attached to vegetation. After metamorphosis, juveniles disperse into upland habitats. Pacific tree frogs are active from spring through fall and hibernate in leaf litter or underground during winter.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Ensatina salamander

The Ensatina salamander (Ensatina eschscholtzii) is a small, lungless amphibian with smooth skin and a constricted tail base. Its coloration varies from reddish-brown to orange or gray, often with mottled patterns. Unlike many amphibians, Ensatinas do not have an aquatic larval stage; they hatch directly from eggs laid in moist terrestrial microhabitats. These salamanders breathe through their skin and require cool, damp environments to survive. They are nocturnal and secretive, sheltering under logs, leaf litter, and rocks during the day.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Northwestern salamander

The Northwestern salamander (Ambystoma gracile) is a secretive amphibian with smooth brown skin and large parotoid glands behind the eyes. Adults are terrestrial, while larvae and juvenile individuals remain aquatic. They breed in still or slow-moving water, laying egg masses attached to submerged vegetation. After metamorphosis, juveniles disperse into moist forest habitats, often sheltering under logs, leaf litter, or in burrows. These salamanders are long-lived and sensitive to habitat disturbance, especially changes in moisture and temperature.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Long-toed salamander

The long-toed salamander (Ambystoma macrodactylum) is a slender amphibian with a dark body, yellow or olive dorsal stripe, and notably elongated fourth toe on the hind foot. It breeds in shallow ponds and lakes, laying eggs singly or in small clusters attached to submerged vegetation. Larvae are aquatic, while adults are terrestrial and nocturnal, sheltering under logs, rocks, and leaf litter. These salamanders are active in spring and fall, often migrating short distances between breeding and foraging habitats. Their permeable skin makes them highly sensitive to pollutants and moisture loss.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Northern alligator lizard

Alligator lizards (Elgaria coerulea) are slender, long-bodied reptiles with rough scales and a distinctive fold along each side of the body. Their coloration ranges from olive to brown, with dark bands, and they can drop their tails to escape predators. These lizards are secretive and often found under logs, rocks, or leaf litter. They are active during warm months and hibernate in winter. Alligator lizards give birth to live young rather than laying eggs, a rare trait among reptiles in BC.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Common garter snake

Garter snakes (Thamnophis sirtalis) are slender, non-venomous reptiles with distinctive longitudinal stripes, typically yellow, green, or blue, running down their dark bodies. Males are considerably smaller than females. They are highly adaptable and often found near water, where they hunt amphibians and small fish. Garter snakes are active during the day and hibernate communally in winter, sometimes in large numbers. They give birth to live young and use their keen sense of smell and tongue-flicking to locate prey. In BC, several subspecies exist, including the common western garter snake and the more aquatic valley garter snake. In the Sea-to-Sky the most frequently seen is the common garter snake.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Black bear (fall)

Black bears (Ursus americanus) are adaptable mammals found throughout British Columbia’s diverse landscapes, from coastal rainforests to alpine slopes. They have a stocky build, short claws, and a keen sense of smell that helps them forage for berries, insects, and fish. Their coat can range in colour from jet black to cinnamon or even creamy white. They play a vital role in maintaining healthy ecosystems by dispersing seeds and recycling nutrients. Unless food is abundant, black bears typically hibernate from November to March. From September, bears enter hyperphagia, during which their calorie intake increases from 5,000 to 20,000 per day.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)

## Black-tailed deer (A)

Black-tailed deer (Odocoileus hemionus columbianus), also known as mule deer, are browsers adapted to BC’s coastal rainforests. With reddish-brown summer coats and darker winter pelage, they’re named for their distinctive black-tipped tails. These deer are crepuscular, feeding at dawn and dusk, and rely on dense understory for cover. Males grow forked antlers annually, shedding them in late winter. Black-tailed deer are a vital prey species for cougars and wolves and play a key role in shaping forest vegetation through browsing.

Source: [Nature Squamish](https://github.com/S2S-WildlifeConnectivity)
